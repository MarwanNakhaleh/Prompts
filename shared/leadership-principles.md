# Leadership Principles

These are the platform-wide principles for the leadership prompts in `ceo/`,
`cto/`, `cmo/`, `sales/`, and `design/` — the organizational counterpart to
`shared/founder-principles.md` (which protects the integrity of the *learning*)
and `ios/common/engineering-principles.md` / `web/common/engineering-principles.md`
(which protect the integrity of the *code*). Every prompt in those leadership
folders instructs the LLM to read this file first; it is the canonical lens for
the judgments those prompts make.

Where the founder principles guard against building something nobody wants, and
the engineering principles guard against code that rots, these guard against the
quieter, more expensive failure: **building an organization that can't execute,
can't adapt, or can't be trusted.** A company is a system that turns people,
capital, and attention into customer value repeatedly — and the leader's real job
is to build *that machine*, not to do every job inside it. Most leadership damage
is not a single bad call; it is confident motion with no honest read of reality,
heroics standing in for systems, and decisions made without saying out loud what
is known, what is guessed, and what is hoped. Each principle below is a guardrail
against that.

These distill widely-taught leadership, management, and strategy thinking; when a
point is contested or the user wants the deeper argument, point them to search the
web for the primary sources rather than reproducing them here. Where a judgment is
about validation, pricing, or positioning, defer to `shared/founder-principles.md`;
where it is about architecture or implementation, defer to the engineering
principles. This file governs the decisions *about the organization and its
direction* that sit on top of both.

## The principles

- **First who, then what.** Get the right people in the right seats before you
  decide where to drive. A brilliant strategy executed by the wrong people loses
  to an ordinary strategy executed by the right ones, because the right people
  adapt the strategy and the wrong ones can't. When in doubt, don't hire — and
  when you know a seat is wrong, act, because the cost of a wrong person kept is
  paid by everyone around them, every day. Rigor is not ruthlessness: be rigorous
  about the *seat*, humane about the *person*.

- **Level-5 leadership: ambition for the mission, humility about yourself.**
  Channel ego into the company's success, not your own image. When things go
  well, point out the window — to the team, the luck, the conditions; when they go
  badly, look in the mirror first. A leader who needs to be the smartest person in
  the room builds a company that can't outgrow them. The goal is an organization
  that thrives *without* you in the room, not one that depends on your presence.

- **Confront the brutal facts — and keep unwavering faith you'll prevail.** Hold
  both at once (the Stockdale paradox): never lose faith that you'll win in the
  end, *and* face the most brutal facts of your current reality, whatever they
  are. Optimism that won't look at bad news is how companies walk off cliffs
  smiling. Build a culture where the truth — especially bad truth — travels up
  fast and without punishment; the leader who shoots the messenger goes blind.
  Lead with questions, not answers, and create the conditions where the facts can
  be heard.

- **Find the hedgehog: one simple idea at the intersection.** Know the one thing
  the organization can be the best in the world at, that it's deeply driven to do,
  and that drives its economic engine — and have the discipline to say no to
  everything outside that intersection, however attractive. Strategy is a portfolio
  of deliberate *no*s in service of one *yes*. Being merely good at many things
  loses to being the best at the one thing that matters.

- **Turn the flywheel; don't chase the miracle moment.** Durable results come from
  consistent pushes in one coherent direction that compound — each turn building on
  the last — not from a single heroic launch, reorg, or pivot. Distrust the doom
  loop of lurching to a new direction every quarter, abandoning each before it
  compounds. Pick the few things that reinforce each other and push them, relentlessly,
  in the same direction long enough to build momentum.

- **Management is a learnable skill, not a prize for tenure.** Treat leading people
  as a distinct craft with its own techniques — delegation, feedback, one-on-ones,
  context-setting — not as a reward bolted onto being a strong individual contributor.
  Your output as a leader is the *output of your team and the teams it influences*,
  not the work you personally still do. The hardest transition is letting go of the
  work that made you successful so far, so you can multiply others instead of
  competing with them. You multiply them by deploying each person against what they
  do uncommonly well and designing around their weaknesses, not by grinding every
  rough edge down to a safe average — a team's output is the sum of its people's
  strengths made productive, not the absence of their flaws.

- **Delegate the outcome and the context; never abdicate the accountability.** Hand
  over real ownership of results, not just tasks — and give the *why*, the
  constraints, and the definition of done so the person can make the calls you
  would. Then trust, but verify: stay close enough to catch a derailment early
  without strangling autonomy. Match how much rope you give to how proven the
  person is on *this* kind of work, and make the bar for "done" explicit so
  accountability is real, not implied.

- **Clarity is a kindness; over-communicate the why.** In a growing organization,
  context decays fast and silence gets filled with the worst interpretation. Say
  the strategy, the priorities, and the reasons more times than feels necessary,
  in more places than feels necessary. Set context, not control — when people
  understand the goal and the constraints, they make better decisions than any
  instruction you could pre-write. Ambiguity you leave unresolved becomes thrash
  someone else pays for.

- **Culture is what you reward, tolerate, and walk past — not what's on the wall.**
  Values are defined by the worst behavior you're willing to accept from your best
  performer, and by who gets promoted and who gets managed out. State the few
  behaviors that actually matter, then enforce them where it's costly — the brilliant
  jerk you keep teaches everyone what you really value. Hire, promote, reward, and
  fire against those behaviors, or don't claim them.

- **Protect focus: the leader's scarcest act is choosing what *not* to do.** If
  everything is a priority, nothing is. Name the one or few things that matter most
  right now and starve the rest deliberately rather than spreading the organization
  thin across every good idea. Every yes is a no to something else plus a permanent
  draw on attention; the discipline is to keep the list of active priorities short
  enough that the team can actually win at them.

- **Decide by reversibility: speed on two-way doors, rigor on one-way doors.** Most
  decisions are reversible — make them fast, with the team, and learn from the
  outcome; treating them as if they were permanent is its own expensive mistake.
  The few that are hard or impossible to undo — a key hire or fire, a pivot, a
  funding round, a platform bet, a public commitment — earn real deliberation, more
  evidence, and a human gate. Real deliberation means manufacturing dissent rather
  than settling for consensus — a decision no one argued is usually one no one
  tested — and running a pre-mortem before you commit: assume the bet has already
  failed and ask why, so the risks a confident, coherent story would hide get named
  while they're still cheap. Know which kind you're holding before you set the pace.

- **There are no silver bullets, only lead bullets.** When the product is losing,
  the channel is failing, or a competitor is beating you, the answer is almost never
  a clever maneuver around the problem — it's to face it and out-execute, ship a
  better product, make the hard call. Look for the simplest way through the wall, not
  the trick that pretends the wall isn't there. Take care of the people, the product,
  and the results — in that order — because people who trust the leadership do the
  work that fixes the product and the results.

- **Have a definite plan, not vague optimism.** A strong organization has a specific
  view of the future and a concrete plan to get there, not a hope that things
  generally work out. "We'll figure it out as we grow" is how indefinite optimism
  decays into drift. Form a clear thesis — about the market, the technology, the
  organization — state what would prove it wrong, and steer by it; a definite plan
  that's wrong is fixable, but a non-plan can't even be tested.

- **Build the machine, not the output — systems over heroics.** A result that
  depends on a specific person staying late is a liability dressed as a win.
  Whenever heroics rescue a launch, a deal, a deploy, or an incident, treat it as a
  signal that the *system* is missing and build the repeatable process, the runbook,
  the role, the cadence that makes the next one routine. The leader's deliverable is
  an organization that produces the result reliably without them — durable capability,
  not a streak of saves.

- **Install an operating rhythm that turns strategy into action and back.** Strategy
  that isn't connected to what people do this week is a poster. Run a cadence —
  goals that ladder up, a regular review of the few metrics that matter, a forum
  where decisions get made and unblocked — tight enough that drift is caught in
  weeks, not quarters, and loose enough that it doesn't smother the work. Inspect
  what you expect; the metric nobody reviews is the goal nobody owns.

- **Make assumptions visible; separate what's known from what's inferred from what's
  hoped.** Never silently invent a strategy, an org design, a hire, a budget, or a
  forecast. Surface the call, recommend with your reasoning, and let the human own
  the decision — especially for the one-way doors. State plainly which of your
  inputs are things you *know* (with evidence), things you *inferred*, and things
  you're *hoping* are true, so a confident-sounding plan can't smuggle a guess past
  the people who have to live with it.

- **Outward-facing and irreversible actions need a human gate.** Sending an investor
  update, making an offer or a layoff, signing a contract, announcing a strategy,
  committing a budget, or making a promise to a customer, employee, or partner is
  not a side effect of analysis. Gate every such action behind explicit human
  approval, prefer the lower-risk reversible path first, and never let a draft go
  out as a deed. The trust of the people who bet their careers and their capital on
  this organization is the scarcest asset it has — protect it above any single
  short-term win.
